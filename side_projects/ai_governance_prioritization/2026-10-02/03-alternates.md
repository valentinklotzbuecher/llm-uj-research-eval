<a name="saferai"></a>
## Evaluating AI Companies' Frontier Safety Frameworks: Methodology and Results

**Lily Stelling, Malcolm Murray, Simeon Campos, and Henry Papadatos. Proposed queue: about 10. Move up from alternate status.**

### Why this could matter

The paper scores 12 frontier-AI safety frameworks against 65 weighted criteria drawn from risk-management practice and adapted to advanced AI. It reports low current scores and identifies recurring weaknesses such as absent quantitative risk tolerances, vague thresholds for pausing development, and incomplete treatment of unknown risks. The key governance question is whether these voluntary frameworks are meaningful accountability mechanisms, especially now that public policy increasingly refers to developer safety frameworks. [Primary paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01166).

This is a good Unjournal target because the output looks precise while depending on contestable construct choices: which criteria belong, how they are weighted, what counts as satisfying them, and whether practices from aviation or nuclear regulation transfer well to frontier AI.

### Attention, separated by type

**Policy-synthesis uptake:** the strongest verified signal is direct citation in the [International AI Safety Report 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026), which is intended to support policymakers and was developed with an expert process involving more than 30 countries and international organisations. The report cites Stelling et al. in its discussion of frontier safety frameworks. This is genuine uptake into an international policy synthesis, but not evidence that the participating governments endorse SaferAI's scores or methodology.

**Institutional involvement:** SaferAI is itself represented among the contributing organisations to the International AI Safety Report, and one SaferAI author served as a chapter lead. Those facts increase proximity to the policy-safety ecosystem, but they are not independent validation of this paper.

**Independent media:** I did not verify major independent press coverage centered on the paper itself. Third-party summaries exist, but they are weaker evidence than the policy-report citation.

**Funding/support:** no paper-specific funder reliance or commissioning relationship was verified in this pass.

### What an Unjournal evaluation could add

A risk-management specialist plus a measurement-validity or auditing expert should examine criterion selection, weights, scoring rules, inter-rater reliability, treatment of missing/ambiguous language, sensitivity to alternative weights, and whether the "peer best-practice" ceiling is normatively meaningful. Company scores should be treated as outputs of a rubric, not direct estimates of real-world safety.

The most valuable reanalysis would show how rankings and substantive conclusions change under plausible alternative criteria and weights, while separating "specific enough to audit" from "actually adequate to control risk." A legal or policy reader could also check how closely the rubric maps onto obligations that matter under California and EU regimes.

**Bottom line:** move this ahead of most alternates. It has actual policy-synthesis uptake and a tractable, auditable methodology. The main risk is that an evaluation merely re-scores the same documents rather than interrogating construct validity.

## Who Evaluates AI's Social Impacts? Mapping Coverage and Gaps in First and Third Party Evaluations

**Anka Reuel, Avijit Ghosh, Jenny Chim, and 32 coauthors. Proposed queue: about 13. Strong lower-cost empirical reserve.**

### Why this could matter

The paper compares first-party model release reports with third-party social-impact evaluations across bias, harmful content, privacy, environment, labor and other dimensions, and supplements the coding with developer interviews. The current version reports 186 first-party reports and 248 third-party evaluation sources. It argues that developer reporting is sparse in several areas while third parties provide broader coverage, but that some important information can only be supplied by developers. [Current arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05613).

This bears directly on transparency mandates, independent evaluator access, and the division of labor between firms and external auditors. It is also close to The Unjournal's own institutional interest in public evaluation.

### Attention, separated by type

**Academic circulation:** the paper was accepted at ICML 2026 and appears in the conference proceedings. [ICML/arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05613). This is substantial field-level attention, but not policy adoption.

**Policy use:** I did not verify a government, regulator, or international-policy report clearly relying on the paper. The governance relevance of the topic should not be reported as evidence of use.

**Media:** no strong independent media pickup was found in this pass.

**Funding/support:** paper-specific funder reliance was not verified.

### What an Unjournal evaluation could add

This is especially suitable for a measurement-methods review. The largest threat is comparability: first-party reports are a defined universe of release documents, while third-party evaluations may have been searched for by impact dimension. If so, a direct average-completeness comparison partly reflects the collection protocol rather than only evaluator effort. The paper should be assessed on the exact sampling frame, search strategy, coding anchors, reliability, missingness, and time trends.

The version history matters. Earlier summaries list 183 third-party sources while the May 30 revision reports 248. A review must freeze the version and explain what changed before reproducing numbers. Developer interviews also deserve separate treatment from the content-coding evidence.

**Bottom line:** good fit and relatively cheap to audit. Keep below work with demonstrated policy use, but consider it for a methodological pilot or an evaluation-infrastructure mini-round.

## Mapping General-Purpose AI Governance in Twenty AI Middle-Power Jurisdictions

**Josephine Schwab, Nathan Naidoo, Ferruccio Barazzutti, Sheryn Lee, and Caio Vieira Machado. Proposed queue: roughly 23. Prefer a data/code audit or synthesis note over a full referee package.**

### Why this could matter

The paper maps GPAI-relevant provisions in 20 jurisdictions across systemic-risk assessment, evaluation and verification, monitoring/prohibitions, and incident reporting. It reports convergence in the kinds of governance mechanisms being created but much less convergence in legal force, including a low share of binding provisions and little binding model-layer evaluation outside the EU. [Primary paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19278).

For middle-power governments, the dataset could be useful as a comparative policy map. But the paper is deliberately descriptive: it does not estimate whether the regimes work, why they emerged, or how they affect developer behavior. That limits the marginal value of a conventional Unjournal evaluation.

### Attention, separated by type

**Institutional promotion:** [Arcadia Impact](https://www.arcadiaimpact.org/research/s26-gpai-mapping) hosts the work as a Summer 2026 AI Governance Taskforce project with The Future Society and says the findings were taken to WAIC 2026. This is author/institution dissemination, not independent uptake.

**Independent/policy media:** AIJurium included the paper in an academic roundup. That shows some specialist visibility but is not evidence of policymaker use.

**Government/funder use:** not verified. The dataset covers governments' rules, but being about government policy is not the same as being used by governments.

### What an Unjournal evaluation could add

If commissioned, focus narrowly on legal-coding validity: inclusion rules, jurisdiction selection, cutoff dates, treatment of soft law, what counts as "binding," duplicate provisions across instruments, inter-coder agreement, and reproducibility from primary legal texts. A legal-comparative methods reviewer could be useful.

The bigger opportunity may be to treat this as infrastructure: verify and reuse the provision-level dataset in a living comparative governance resource rather than spend a full evaluation budget on a mainly descriptive article.

**Bottom line:** useful source material, modest priority for a standalone evaluation. Upgrade if a middle-power government, international coordination project, or funder identifies a concrete comparative question that depends on the coding.
