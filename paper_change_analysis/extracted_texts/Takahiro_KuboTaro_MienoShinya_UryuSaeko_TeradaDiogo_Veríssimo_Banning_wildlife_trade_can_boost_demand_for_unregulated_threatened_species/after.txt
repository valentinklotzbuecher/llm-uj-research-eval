1 Banning wildlife trade can boost demand for unregulated threatened species
2
3 Abstract
4 Regulations on the harvest and use of natural resources might have unintended spillover impacts
5 beyond the policy targets. Banning wildlife trade is an immediate measure to protect species from
6 overexploitation. However, few causal inference studies have investigated the consequences of
7 wildlife trade bans. We use the synthetic difference-in-differences approach to causal inference
8 based on a 10-year online auction dataset to explore whether trade bans on three threatened species
9 in Japan–giant water bugs, Tokyo salamanders, and golden venus chub–have spillover impacts on
10 trades of substitutable non-banned species. We find conservation side effects of wildlife trade bans,
11 leading to an increase in trade volume of non-banned species in each taxon. Our results raise
12 concerns about the unintended consequences of trade bans and restate the importance of further
13 efforts concerning consumer research, monitoring and enforcement beyond the target species,
14 while minimizing costs by applying machine learning technologies and enhancing international
15 cooperation.

17 1 Main
18 The unsustainable wildlife trade is a major threat to biodiversity conservation1.
19 Commercial wildlife trade is a multi-billion dollar activity that supports millions of livelihoods
20 worldwide2, 3, but unsustainable trade causes population decline suggesting a sixth mass
21 extinction4, 5, 6. The recent rise in internet usage and the move of online trade can fuel wildlife
22 trades through online e-commerce platforms and social media, increasing the risk of illegal
23 and/or unsustainable trade across the globe 7, 8, 9, 10.
24 To mitigate this risk, trade bans at both the domestic and international levels are one of the
25 most common and immediate measures for governments to manage wildlife trade11. There is
26 no doubt that trade bans play substantial roles toward sustainable wildlife trade. However, the
27 introduction and existence of the bans might lead to unintended and/or negative consequences
28 for species conservation12, 13, 14, 15. For example, the introduction of the Convention on
29 International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) trade ban
30 increased the trade volume and price of wildlife and the products12, 13. This subsequently
31 activated the underground market16, 17. Furthermore, the trade ban affected species distributions
32 and ecological threats18, 19.
33 However, most studies investigating the effect of trade ban policies have focused solely
34 on banned species12, while the scarcity of comprehensive datasets on wildlife trade have
35 resulted in limited policy evaluation studies20. This indicates that governments and researchers
36 might have overlooked the risk of unintended spillover impacts on unregulated (i.e., legally
37 tradable) threatened species caused by trade bans. As disentangling the cause-effect
38 relationships of policy interventions is at the heart of effective development of conservation
39 policies and regulations9, 20, 21, rigorous policy evaluations identifying causality are required.
40 This study explores the impacts of the trade ban regulation implemented in February 2020
41 in Japan22, 23, 24—one of the largest wildlife trade countries—by employing 10 years of online
42 auction data. We answer the question “Do trade bans have spillover impacts on the demand for
43 non-banned species?” by applying the recently developed causal inference approach, synthetic
44 difference-in-differences (SDID)25. Advantages of the method enable us to identify policy
45 impacts by considering global/national impacts, such as the COVID-19 pandemic (see Methods
46 for details), and to provide extensive insights into the spillover mechanisms and raise caution
47 to the fact that wildlife trade bans can present unintended dangers to other threatened species.

107 3 Discussion
108 Our findings show conservation side effects of wildlife trade bans on non-banned
109 threatened species as substitutes of banned species. Bans on the three policy targeted species–
110 giant water bugs, Tokyo salamanders, and golden venus chub–led to an increase in the trade
111 volumes of non-banned species in each taxon, indicating that banning wildlife trade can bring
112 unintended dangers to other threatened species.
113 The findings have several important insights into consequences. First, demand caused by
114 the trade ban can reach non-banned threatened species in countries where the ban is
115 implemented. For example, a substitute for the giant water bugs Cybister chinensis is
116 designated as vulnerable in the Japanese Red List and is extinct in five prefectures26. This effect
117 potentially harms conservation of many legally tradable threatened species currently sold
118 online by driving up demand for the alternatives, which can activate wild harvest. This concern
119 is not limited to the country implementing trade bans. Spillover effects are not necessarily
120 limited to native species; they are also applicable to exotic species, such as giant water bugs,
121 from other countries. An increase in exotic species trade can increase overexploitation risk in
122 source countries and lead to population declines unless appropriate management is
123 implemented27. Source countries, often developing countries, may face difficulties in meeting
124 additional management needs because of the trade ban, as they often struggle to implement
125 robust natural resource governance4.
126 In addition to overexploitation risk, the imports and breeding of exotic species stimulated
127 by the policy change raise concerns about the potential impacts of invasive species if exotic
128 pet species are (un)intentionally released27, 28. Moreover, the more often exotic species in
129 closely related taxa to the banned species are imported, the higher the risk of disease outbreaks
130 that could affect the banned species29. Considering these concerns, we highlight the possibility
131 of the existence of a feedback loop in which trade bans may accelerate threats to biodiversity
132 conservation. Trade bans can stimulate demand for non-banned species, triggering new
133 regulations, and stimulate demand for other non-banned species, thus creating a self-
134 perpetuating loop. Indeed, the commercial trade of Cybister chinensis, for which we detected
135 the positive spillover effects from the big water bug in this study, has been prohibited since
136 January 2023. As these processes can occur with other feedback challenges like Anthropogenic
137 Allee Effect13, these multiple feedback loops may accelerate human-induced population decline
138 of both banned and non-banned species6, 30.
6

139 To mitigate the risk of the negative consequences, we note some policy and
140 management implications. We first call for understanding market dynamics and recommend
141 the use of behavioral science insights to manage demand prior to introducing a trade ban15, 31,
142 32. Our findings concerning heterogeneity of the effects across taxa note that an initial goal of
143 interventions should be to reduce the demand for the species to be banned, therefore reducing
144 the magnitude of any potential spillover. For example, the demand for and public interest in
145 giant water bugs were considerably higher than the other species (see Fig. 1 and Fig. S1), which
146 possibly led to higher potential leakage of the demand for the non-banned species following
147 the ban. Following this, demand management should be aimed at redirecting any spillover
148 demand towards legal and sustainable sources, either from well-managed wild populations or
149 captive breeds, thus minimizing any threats to biodiversity. Identifying these potential
150 substitute sources requires a detailed understanding not only of the conservation status and
151 trade sustainability of different wildlife populations and species but also of consumer
152 motivations and preferences, which are often context-dependent and culture-specific.
153 The complexity of understanding substitutability has been underlined by ongoing
154 debates on the potential of farmed products as substitutes for wild-sourced products33, 34. This
155 uncertainty regarding an issue that has received substantial research attention shows the
156 importance of further research to avoid situations wherein substitutes are deemed either
157 biologically unsuitable or not desirable by consumers33. This need for planning highlights the
158 systemic risks of reactive bans—often the case in wildlife trade—imposed on a short notice
159 and often without detailed knowledge of the systems they intended to influence. Additionally,
160 we highlight the importance of understanding the mechanisms behind the supply side of trade.
161 Supply volume can be affected by characteristics of wildlife and wildlife products such as
162 accessibility and price elasticity. Due to the relatively low capture cost for the taxa in this study,
163 their transaction volumes can be affected by the policy change35. Policy makers and researchers
164 need to contribute to the development of market-based approaches36, 37, for example, increasing
165 the transaction costs of the potential spillover species via regulations.
166 To develop effective interventions to manage wildlife trade, further efforts concerning
167 monitoring the volume, composition, and prices of species trade are needed. Specifically, our
168 findings insist that the monitoring should be extended to unregulated threatened species.
169 However, as demonstrated by the case of Japan, where the conservation budget has increased
170 only marginally while the number of protected species has been increasing38, it is essential to
171 enhance monitoring efficiency and management capacity as conservation practices have faced
172 substantial financial shortages39. There are several ways to improve monitoring efficiency and
7

173 management capacity. The latest computer science technology can, for example, be utilized to
174 identify traded species40, 41. Thus far, species identification has been challenging, even on
175 online trade platforms, since species are traded using not only species names, but also common
176 names and codified language designed to be understood only by those within specific pet owner
177 and trader networks42. Our findings based on datasets processed using natural language
178 processing (NLP) can, in part, showcase benefits of the technology.
179 Furthermore, the findings regarding cross-country spillovers from other countries calls
180 for international cooperation beyond what currently occurs under CITES. We suggest the
181 development of a database comprising the banned and non-banned traded/tradable species with
182 common names in multiple languages; this can help disseminate information on policy
183 interventions for the species across countries. Overcoming language barriers in conservation
184 science and practice43 will better identify species traded across borders and ensure more reliable
185 trade monitoring.
186 At a time when biodiversity is being lost at an unprecedented rate, evidence-based
187 policy evaluation is essential for effective measures. Policy evaluations ignoring spillover
188 effects might overstate the benefits of trade ban policies in conservation. Our evidence raises
189 concerns about the unintended consequences of wildlife trade bans through a feedback loop of
190 spillover demand, which can threaten conservation status of non-banned species. This
191 challenge is not limited to legislation aimed at conservation. Banning wildlife trade to cope
192 with wildlife disease, which has been discussed during the COVID-19 pandemic, might also
193 stimulate demand and lead to species extinction via this mechanism14, 44, 45. The introduction
194 of legislation requires further consumer research, monitoring and enforcement that goes
195 beyond the species targeted by the policy, while minimizing the costs vias the application of
196 modern technologies and enhancing international cooperation.

198 4 Methods
199 4.1 Policy background
200 The Japanese government has enforced the law, “The Act on Conservation of
201 Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (ACES)”, to conserve endangered species (for
202 details, see the description by the Ministry of the Environment, Japan:
203 https://www.env.go.jp/nature/kisho/kisei/en/species/index.html). Under the ACES, threatened
204 wildlife species in Japan are designated as Nationally Endangered Species of Wildlife Fauna
205 and Flora. There is a ban on capture, trade, transfer, and export of these designated species.
206 Further, one is not allowed to display or advertise such species for commercial trade. This
207 regulation applies not only to living species, including both wild-taken and captive-bred, but
208 also to dead biological specimens and products made from the designated species.
209 With the recent increase in demand for wildlife trade, there is a growing concern that
210 mass capture for commercial purposes can cause species extinction. Particularly, semi-natural
211 ecosystem species (for example water bugs, amphibians, and freshwater fishes) are now at risk
212 of species extinction due to this commercial use in addition to the existing risk of habitat
213 destruction. To regulate the commercial use of the species without discouraging the
214 conservation activities, the Japanese government amended the law and created a new
215 framework, called “Class II Designated Nationally Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and
216 Flora”. On February 10, 2020, the regulation of this framework was introduced on three
217 species—giant water bugs, Tokyo salamanders, and golden venus chub—by considering their
218 ecological status and characteristics. Accordingly, under this regulation, the capture, trade, and
219 transfer of the three species for commercial activities have been prohibited since February 10,
220 2020 (For details, see the press release on the policy amendment:
221 https://www.env.go.jp/press/107622.html).
222
223 4.2 Identification strategy
224 This study analyzed the introduction of the trade ban policy on the three species in Japan
225 using online auction data to answer the question, “Do trade bans have spillover impacts on the
226 demand for non-banned species?”. We used SDID to estimate the causal impact of the wildlife
227 trade bans on demand for non-banned species. SDID has several advantages over previous
228 causal inference approaches25. For example, unlike synthetic control methods (SCM), SDID
229 controls for individual specific fixed effects and constructs weights allowing for difference in
230 the dependent variable prior to the treatment. Unlike difference-in-differences (DID), SDID
9

231 weights observations to construct more credible synthetic control than DID, making it more
232 plausible to satisfy the parallel trend assumption, which is crucial for unbiased identification
233 of the treatment effect. These advantages enable us to identify policy impacts by considering
234 common shock, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, while this modelling flexibility
235 comes at the small cost of statistical efficiency when either SCM or DID is correctly specified,
236 SDID is more robust than SCM and DID25.
237 In this paper, we control for seasonal trend in the SDID framework (typically more trades are
238 happening in the summer than winter; see Fig 1-2, for example). It has been shown that the
239 two-step procedure developed by Kranz (2023) performs better than the original SDID method
240 when there are other controls (here, seasonal dummies) other than individual fixed effects, year
241 fixed effects, and the treatment variable25, 46. The two-step procedure works as follows:

256 4.3 Data
257 We used a 10-year dataset from Japan’s leading online auction platform Yahoo! Auction
258 (https://auctions.yahoo.co.jp/). The dataset included the nine years prior to the ban (February
10

259 2011–2020) and one year after the ban (February 2020–2021). The data was downloaded from
260 the publicly accessible mirroring website Aucfan (https://aucfan.com/). The number of
261 transactions composed of each taxon’s banned, potential spillover, and control species is
262 333349, 22766, and 91879 for water bugs, salamanders, and freshwater fish, respectively (see
263 Table S3 and Datasets in the SI Appendix for details). This dataset includes the following
264 variables: traded dates, titles, traded categories, and final auction prices of each traded product.
265 Traded categories, such as amphibian, were given by the auction website (i.e.,
266 https://auctions.yahoo.co.jp/list1/0-all.html). We applied NLP to extract the traded species’
267 names using the information on the titles and categories; then, we manually confirmed the
268 names of the banned and potential spillover species.
269 The outcomes of the identifications are the potential spillover species’ trade volumes to
270 clarify the spillover demands for non-banned species. The potential spillover species (i.e.,
271 treated units) were chosen based on three criteria as we suppose they are substitutable species
272 relative to the banned species. First, we selected species expressed by the same Japanese names
273 with those of the banned species but legally tradable in the market. These species were not
274 under the trade ban because they were biologically different from the banned species (i.e., they
275 had different scientific names from the banned species). Second, to find the similar
276 characteristics through a lens of human preference and searchability, we queried Google Trends
277 for the banned three species and sales in Japanese for the 10-year data period, and used related
278 queries through the R package “gtrendsR”48 (see Note S1 for details). Third, if no candidate
279 species were available after considering the two criteria for each banned species, the three
280 phylogenetically closest species in the market were selected using the phylogeny with reference
281 to iNaturalist (https://www.inaturalist.org/) and OneZoom (https://www.onezoom.org/). As
282 previous studies exploring substitutability in wildlife trade have mainly focused on traditional
283 Chinese medicine49, it is not possible to judge how well these criteria capture the similarities
284 between banned and potential spillover species to consider substitutability. However, these
285 procedures concerning criteria selection do not overestimate the spillover impacts even if there
286 is misassignment concerning species in the SDID models (see Note S2 in the SI Appendix for
287 details). Following this procedure, three species were chosen as potential spillover species for
288 each banned species (see Table S1 in the SI Appendix for details), whereas we also conducted
289 sensitivity analyses focusing on a species in each category to confirm the findings (see Table
290 S4 in the SI Appendix for details).
291 The control units were the trades in the same categories given by the auction website as
292 those of the banned species, excluding those of the potential spillover species. For example,
11

293 the control units to identify the spillover effects of banning Tokyo salamander were trades in
294 the amphibian category on the auction website excluding the trades of potential spillover
295 species (Hynobius nebulosus, Hynobius nigrescens, and Hynobius lichenatus). Given the large
296 volume of the datasets, not all species in the control units were identified by a scientific name;
297 therefore, as a robustness check, we also analyzed the datasets with only the control units
298 identified by the scientific names in the same taxonomic families as the trade banned species
299 (see Fig. S3-4, S7-8 and Datasets in the SI Appendix for the details).
300 Considering seasonality and the balanced panel requirements of SDID, the data analysis
301 was compiled on a quarterly basis, starting on the day the policy was implemented, February
302 10, 2020. Expressly, the pre-treatment period is from February 10, 2011, to February 9, 2020
303 and the post-treated period is from February 10, 2020 to February 9, 2021. The trade volume
304 of each species was aggregated by quarter. Additionally, as a robustness check, we conducted
305 sensitivity and placebo analyses with different datasets to confirm our findings (see Table S3-
306 4 in the SI Appendix for details). Because of the discovery of new salamander species during
307 this research, the species were aggregated into the oldest taxa50 (see Table S2 in the SI
308 Appendix for the details).

117 Note S2. The implications of treatment-control designations
118 The biggest challenge in identifying the impact of the regulation is to determine which
119 control or treatment group non-banned species belong to. Here, we show that group mis-
120 designation would result in the underestimation of treatment effect using an illustrative
121 example. Below we assume that the spillover effect to other species should be positive since
122 potential buyers can use the money they would have used for the banned species to other
123 species when a species is banned.
124 Suppose you have 8 species: 4 treated and 4 untreated. Further suppose that the
125 treatment effect is 50 trades for all the four treated species. When the treatment status is
126 correctly assigned, the estimated treatment effect would be (4 * 50 – 4 * 0) / 4 = 50. However,
127 suppose one of the treated species is assigned to the control group and one of the untreated
128 species is assigned to the treated group. Then, the estimated impact would be [(3 * 50 + 0) – (3
129 * 0 + 50)] / 4 = 25. If two of the treated species are assigned to the control group and two of
130 the untreated species are assigned to the treated group, then the estimated impact would be [(2
131 * 50 +2 * 0) – (2 * 0 + 2 * 50)] / 4 = 0. The impact of treatment can be estimated to be negative.
132 If three of the treated species are assigned to the control group and three of the untreated species
133 are assigned to the treated group, then the estimated impact would be [(1 * 50 + 3 * 0) – (1 * 0
134 + 3 * 50)] / 4 = -25. It is possible that some species are misassigned in this study. That is, we
135 are likely to have a negative bias on our estimation of the impact of the regulation.
136 This implies that if we find a statistically significant positive treatment effect, then it is
137 additional strong evidence that the impact of regulation is indeed positive. However, when we
138 find a negative or statistically insignificant positive treatment, then that does not necessarily
139 mean there was no positive spillover effect because of the inherent negative bias described
140 above.

173 Table S6. Summary of placebo analyses.
174 We conducted 100 rounds of placebo analyses for each dataset type of taxonomy (six total combinations) to test if there any tendency to over-
175 reject the null hypothesis of zero treatment effects. We first eliminate all the treated species from the sample. For each round, we then randomly
176 selected three species as treated units, and then the rest of the species were designated as the control group (all the samples in the placebo
177 analyses come from the control group of our main analyses). Finally, we applied SDID analyses to the data to estimate the impact of the fake
178 treatment and test if the fake treatment has zero impacts at the 5% significant level. The table below shows the results of the 100 rounds of
179 placebo analyses. As you can see in the table, for most of the combinations, the null hypothesis was rejected around five times (5% of 100
180 rounds). These results show that the SDID approach does not tend to excessively reject the null hypothesis of zero treatment effect of treatment
181 that should indeed have zero impacts.
